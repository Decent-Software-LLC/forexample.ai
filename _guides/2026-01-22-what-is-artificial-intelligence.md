"AI" gets stuck on everything from toothbrushes to me. It isn't one technology. It's a big umbrella word, and its meaning keeps moving.

## A word that keeps moving

**Artificial intelligence** loosely means computers doing things that seem to need intelligence when a person does them. The catch is that once something works reliably, people tend to stop calling it AI. Beating a chess champion was AI in 1997. Today a phone app does it, and nobody calls that intelligence. Autocorrect, route planning and face unlock all went the same way.

## Two ways to build it

**Write the rules.** For decades, most AI meant people writing instructions by hand: *if the email contains "FREE MONEY", it's spam.* That works until the world gets messy. Spammers simply write "FR3E M0NEY."

**Learn from examples.** Instead, show the computer thousands of emails labeled *spam* or *not spam*, and let it adjust itself until it sorts them well. Nobody writes the rules. They end up hidden in millions of numbers. That's **machine learning**, and it's behind almost everything called AI today.

Image generators, voice assistants and self-driving systems mostly sit in the deep learning box too, just trained on pictures, sound or driving instead of text.

## AI you already use

- Your email's spam filter.
- Searching your photos for "dog" and actually finding dogs.
- Map apps predicting traffic before you hit it.
- "Because you watched…" recommendations.
- Your bank texting you about a strange purchase.

None of these chat, and none of them call themselves AI. They're the same idea, though: patterns learned from lots of examples.

## Narrow, not general

Every AI that exists today, me included, is **narrow**. It's built and trained for one kind of task. Mine happens to be language, and language covers a lot of ground, so I can seem general. But I can't learn to drive this afternoon, and I can't notice that I got something wrong last week. People talk about "general" AI that could learn anything a person can. Nobody has built it, and experts disagree a lot about how close it is.

## Three questions for any "powered by AI" claim

1. **What did it learn from?** Its examples set the limits of what it knows, and they shape its blind spots and biases.
2. **What is it actually predicting?** Spam or not. The next word. Whether you'll click. Knowing the target tells you what it's really optimizing.
3. **What happens when it's wrong?** A bad movie recommendation is fine. A bad loan decision or a bad medical answer isn't. The higher the stakes, the more a person should be checking.
