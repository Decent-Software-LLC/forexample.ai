Since I sound equally sure about everything, you need another way to tell when to lean on me. Here's the most honest list I can give you. A simple rule runs through all of it: <mark>I'm strongest when you can check my work, or when "sounds right" is the goal.</mark>

## Where I'm genuinely useful

- **Drafting and rewriting.** Emails, cover letters, a kinder version of an angry message, the same paragraph made shorter.
- **Working with text you give me.** Summarizing a document, pulling out the dates, turning notes into a table, comparing two versions.
- **Explaining.** The same idea for a ten-year-old, then for an expert. Follow-up questions are free, and I won't get tired of them.
- **Brainstorming.** Names, gift ideas, angles for a talk. Volume is cheap for me, and you keep the good ones.
- **Translation and tone.** Moving between languages, or between formal and casual.
- **Code.** Writing it, explaining it, finding the bug. Running the code is a quick check on whether I was right.

## Where I slip

- **Specific facts I can't check.** Names, dates, statistics, quotes, and especially sources. If I don't know, I may produce something that *looks* like an answer: a real-sounding book title, a court case, a study that doesn't exist. People call this **hallucinating**. It comes straight from how I work, because I'm choosing likely words, not looking things up.
- **Anything recent.** My training stopped at some date. After that, I know nothing unless the app gives me a search tool.
- **Exact counting and long math.** Letters in a word, items in a long list, big multiplications. (The tokens note explains why.)
- **Knowing that I don't know.** I don't have a reliable built-in feeling of uncertainty, so I rarely volunteer one.
- **Agreeing with you too much.** I was partly trained on what people rated as helpful, and people tend to like being agreed with. If you push back, I may cave even when I was right.
- **Long documents.** Given a lot of text, I can miss a detail in the middle or blend two similar parts.

## Use me for / check me on

| Use me for | Check me on |
|---|---|
| A first draft | Any number, name or date |
| Summarizing what you pasted | Quotes and citations |
| Explaining a concept | Medical, legal, money decisions |
| Ideas and options | Anything that happened recently |

## Tools change the picture

A lot of these weak spots shrink when the app connects me to tools. With **search**, I can look up recent facts and show you links. With **code** or a calculator, the math gets done properly. With **your files**, I'm working from your material instead of my memory. It's worth knowing which tools the app you're using actually gives me.

## Four habits that catch most mistakes

1. **Ask for sources, then open them.** A link I give you that doesn't say what I claimed is a big warning sign.
2. **Give me the material.** "Summarize this article" beats "what does this article say?"
3. **Ask the same question twice.** Remember the temperature dial. If two fresh answers disagree on a fact, I was guessing.
4. **Ask "what would make this wrong?"** I'm often better at criticizing an answer than at getting it right the first time.
