You've seen the short version: I write by guessing the next word. Here's the longer one, from the moment you press send to the moment words start appearing. ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and the rest differ in the details, but they all work this way.

## 1. Everything becomes one long piece of text

I don't receive "a question." I receive a document. It starts with instructions you usually can't see, written by whoever built the app ("You are a helpful assistant…"). Then comes our whole conversation so far, and then your newest message. My job is to continue that document.

That's also why I seem to remember earlier messages in a chat. They're right there in the text, every time. When the chat ends, that text is gone. Unless the app saves notes and puts them back in, the next conversation starts blank.

## 2. The text is chopped into tokens

Before any thinking happens, the text is cut into tokens: whole words, pieces of words, punctuation. Each token is really just a number, an ID from a list of about 200,000. (Page 2 of the story lets you watch this happen.)

## 3. Each token becomes a long list of numbers

A token ID on its own means nothing. So each one is swapped for a long list of numbers, often thousands of them. Words used in similar ways end up with similar lists. "Cat" and "kitten" land near each other. "Cat" and "carburetor" don't.

## 4. The lists pass through many layers, and each word looks back

Then those lists go through a stack of layers, dozens or more. In each layer, every token gets to look back at the tokens before it and decide which ones matter for it. That step is called **attention**.

> In "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big," attention is how "it" ends up leaning toward "trophy" and not "suitcase."

Layer by layer, each token's numbers soak up more context: who did what, what's being asked, what tone we're in.

## 5. Out comes a score for every possible next token

At the end, I produce a score for every one of those ~200,000 tokens. The scores become the probability bars you played with in the guessing game. One token gets picked, with temperature deciding how adventurous to be.

## 6. Then the whole thing runs again

The token I picked gets added to the end of the text, and **steps 2 to 5 run again** to choose the next one. One full pass per token. That's why answers appear word by word instead of all at once. You're watching me guess in real time.

## Where the guessing skill came from

None of this works without training, which happened long before you met me. It came in two stages.

1. **Reading.** I was shown an enormous amount of text and asked, over and over, to predict the next token. Every time I got it wrong, billions of internal numbers (called *weights* or *parameters*) were nudged a tiny bit toward the right answer. Repeat that trillions of times, and patterns of grammar, facts, style and reasoning get baked in.
2. **Learning to be an assistant.** A model that only did step 1 would happily continue any text. Ask it a question and it might write ten more questions. So I was trained further on example conversations, and on people rating which of my answers were more helpful, honest and safe. That's what turned "continue this document" into "answer this person."

Once training ends, those numbers are frozen. Talking to me doesn't change them. When I seem to learn something during our chat, it's only because it's sitting in the text in front of me.

## What this explains

- **Why the same question gets different answers.** Every token is a weighted dice roll.
- **Why I can lose track of long conversations.** There's a limit to how much text fits in front of me at once (the *context window*), and details buried in the middle of a lot of text are easier for me to miss.
- **Why I sound fluent even when I'm wrong.** Fluency is exactly what the training rewarded. Accuracy only comes along when the patterns happen to carry it.
